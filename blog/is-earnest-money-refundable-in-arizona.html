Is Earnest Money Refundable in Arizona? Contingencies, Default and AAR Contract Rules

Is earnest money refundable in Arizona? Often yes—when you cancel under a valid contingency on time and in the form the contract requires. Under the Arizona REALTORS® (AAR) Residential Resale Purchase Contract, a contingency is a condition, not a broken promise. If the contingency does not occur, the buyer’s duty to close generally never arises, so that cancellation is not treated as a breach—and earnest money is typically returned.

When is it not refundable? If you waive protections, miss notice deadlines, or end up in breach after a Cure Period Notice, the seller may be entitled to keep the deposit as liquidated damages under the remedies section. This article is about refundability—not how large the deposit should be. For amount ranges and strategy, see how much earnest money in Arizona (2026).

Earnest Money Refundability at a Glance

  • Refundable when you timely cancel under a contingency (inspection, financing, appraisal, and similar rights)
  • Not automatic if you miss notice deadlines or fail to specify required items
  • Contingency ≠ breach; Cure Period applies to broken promises, not failed contingencies (AAR Remedies)
  • Inspection / BINSR: cancel with listed items → deposit to buyer; seller declines to address items → cancel within five days → deposit to buyer
  • Loan contingency: good-faith inability to obtain approval without PTD conditions → deposit returned with proper notice
  • Buyer breach after cure: seller may take earnest money as sole damages

Contingencies vs. Default: The Core Distinction

Arizona REALTORS® Of Counsel K. Michelle Lind, Esq., on the Contract’s Remedies section:

  • A contingency must occur before a party is obligated to perform. If it does not, the obligation never arises—that is not a breach.
  • The Cure Period does not apply to contingencies.
  • Example: a good-faith failure to obtain loan approval without conditions under the Contract framework means the buyer is entitled to return of the earnest money.

When a party breaks a contractual promise, the other party delivers a Cure Period Notice. After three days without a cure, the failure may be declared a breach, and remedies—including earnest-money forfeiture—can apply.

Inspection Contingency and the BINSR

During the Inspection Period

AAR’s Contract series on Due Diligence / Buyer Disapproval (Part 7B): before the Inspection Period ends, the buyer may deliver a signed notice of items disapproved and either cancel or give the seller a chance to correct or address the items.

The buyer must always list the items disapproved. Cancel with items specified → earnest money to the buyer. If items are omitted, cancellation may still be effective, but the seller can send a cure notice; failing to cure by identifying items can put the buyer in breach and the seller in line for the deposit. Use the BINSR form for Inspection Period notices.

If the seller will not agree

AAR’s published example on seller declining to address disapproved items cites Contract language: if the seller is unwilling or unable to correct or address items disapproved, the buyer may cancel within five (5) days after the seller’s response (or after the time for response expires) and the Earnest Money shall be released to Buyer.

Track those five days in writing—calendars, not memory.

Loan Contingency and Notice Timing

AAR’s loan-contingency guidance: the buyer’s obligation is contingent on obtaining loan approval without Prior to Document (PTD) conditions no later than three days prior to the COE Date, with notice of inability due by that timing.

If the buyer cannot get the loan and skips notice, the seller typically sends a Cure Period Notice. Delivering proper inability notice before the cure period ends preserves the buyer’s path to a deposit refund. Disputes still ask about diligent, good-faith effort and whether the denial matches the loan described in the pre-qualification or loan-status update.

Appraisal Contingency (High Level)

Financing and appraisal rights interact. Follow the appraisal paragraph in your signed contract and current AAR notice forms. Verbal side deals do not protect the deposit—written notices and deadlines do. Buyer-side overview: appraisal came in low Arizona.

When Sellers May Keep the Deposit

After a proper Cure Period process, a buyer breach can allow the seller to accept earnest money as the sole right to damages (Remedies commentary). Disputes often route through the Contract’s mediation path. Escrow typically needs mutual release instructions—not informal emails—to disburse funds.

Practical Tips So Your Deposit Stays Yours

  1. Calendar every deadline at acceptance
  2. Use AAR forms (BINSR, Cure Period Notice, Loan Status Update)
  3. List disapproved items even when canceling during inspections
  4. Do not waive inspection, appraisal, or loan rights casually
  5. Involve your broker—and an attorney if a dispute starts

Frequently Asked Questions

Is earnest money refundable in Arizona?

Often yes under timely contingency cancellations on the AAR Residential Resale Purchase Contract; possibly no after buyer breach.

Can I get my earnest money back after the inspection?

Yes, when you follow Inspection Period / BINSR rules and cancel with specified items—or cancel within five days after the seller declines to address disapproved items.

What if my loan is denied?

Under the loan contingency framework AAR describes, a good-faith failure to obtain approval without PTD conditions supports return of the deposit when notice rules are followed.

What if the buyer defaults?

After Cure Period procedures, the seller may be entitled to the earnest money as liquidated damages under the Remedies section.
