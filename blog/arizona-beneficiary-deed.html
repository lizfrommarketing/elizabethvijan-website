What Is a Beneficiary Deed in Arizona? How It Works, and Beneficiary Deed vs. Trust

What is a beneficiary deed in Arizona? It's a deed, authorized by A.R.S. 33-405, that names who will receive your home when you die. Until then, nothing changes: you keep full ownership, you can sell or refinance without the beneficiary's signature, and you can revoke or replace the deed at any time.

What's the catch? The deed must be signed and recorded with the county recorder before your death, and the property passes subject to any mortgage or lien on it. It covers only the property described, which is why many owners compare it with a living trust.

Data note: Rules come from Arizona Revised Statutes 33-405 and the Arizona House summary of SB 1479 (2026). General information, not legal advice. Talk with an Arizona estate planning attorney before recording any deed.

How a Beneficiary Deed Works

Under A.R.S. 33-405:

  • Takes effect at death: the deed must expressly say it is effective on the owner's death
  • Encumbrances follow the property: mortgages, deeds of trust and liens stay attached
  • Multiple beneficiaries are allowed: as joint tenants with right of survivorship, tenants in common or other valid tenancies
  • Separate property by default: unless the deed says otherwise, the beneficiary receives it as separate property, not community property
  • Successor beneficiaries: you can name a backup and state when that person's interest vests
  • If no beneficiary survives you: the deed is void, unless it provides otherwise

Recording Is Not Optional

  • The deed is valid only if executed and recorded in the county where the property sits before the owner's death
  • An unrecorded deed found in a drawer doesn't work as a beneficiary deed

Changing Your Mind

  • Revoke anytime by executing and recording a revocation before death
  • Latest deed wins: if you record more than one, the last one recorded before death controls
  • A will doesn't override it: a properly recorded beneficiary deed is not revoked by a will
  • No beneficiary sign-off: the beneficiary's signature, consent or notice is not required during your lifetime

Married Couples and Joint Owners

If you own as joint tenants with right of survivorship or community property with right of survivorship:

  • A deed signed by all surviving owners transfers at the last owner's death
  • A deed signed by fewer than all owners is valid only if the last surviving owner signed it
  • The surviving owner's rights come first

Beneficiary Deed vs. Living Trust

Beneficiary Deed

  • Covers: one described property
  • Managed by: you, until death
  • Setup: one recorded deed
  • Changes: record a new deed or a revocation

Living Trust

  • Covers: the assets you place in the trust
  • Managed by: a trustee you name
  • Setup: a trust document, plus retitling assets into it
  • Changes: amend the trust

You don't always have to choose. A.R.S. 33-405 allows a beneficiary deed to name the trustee of a trust, even a revocable one. An attorney can tell you which fits your family and assets.

What's New in 2026: Signing and Recording

Arizona's SB 1479 added protections that apply when you sign and record a deed, per the House bill summary:

  • Notary thumbprint: signers of real property documents place a right thumbprint in the notary's journal
  • Photo ID to record in person or at a recorder's kiosk (escrow, title companies and attorneys are exempt submitters)
  • Class 5 felony for knowingly recording a forged or false real property document

When Selling a Home With a Beneficiary Deed

  • Living owner selling: the beneficiary deed doesn't block a sale. The beneficiary's signature isn't required
  • After the owner's death: the title company will tell the beneficiary what it needs to insure a sale. Plan for that paperwork early
  • Taxes: inherited property raises basis questions; see our guide to capital gains tax on a home sale in Arizona and talk with a tax professional

FAQ: Arizona Beneficiary Deeds

What is a beneficiary deed in Arizona?

A recorded deed under A.R.S. 33-405 that transfers your property at your death.

Can a beneficiary deed be revoked in Arizona?

Yes, by recording a revocation before death. The last-recorded deed controls.

Is a beneficiary deed better than a trust in Arizona?

It depends. A deed covers one property; a trust can hold many assets.

Does the beneficiary have to sign a beneficiary deed?

No. You can still sell or refinance.
